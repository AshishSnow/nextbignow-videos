There's a strip of water just twenty-one miles wide. One out of every five barrels of oil on Earth used to pass through it. And for months now, it's been almost shut. [dramatic] So what happens when the world's oil highway closes?

It's called the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar, the UAE. Their tankers all have to squeeze through this one gap to reach the rest of the world. Before the war, that was about twenty million barrels a day. And most of it was headed to Asia.
---
Then, in late February, a war involving Iran broke out, and ships in the strait started getting hit. Within weeks, oil jumped to around a hundred and eighteen dollars a barrel. Qatar, one of the biggest gas exporters on the planet, stopped shipping. Countries released four hundred million barrels from their emergency reserves. And it still wasn't enough.
---
Here's what that looked like for regular people. The Philippines declared a national energy emergency and put government offices on a four-day week. Slovenia became the first EU country to ration fuel, fifty liters a day per driver. In parts of California, gas topped six dollars a gallon. And Spirit Airlines, already struggling, shut down for good as jet fuel soared.
---
So where are we now? On some days lately, only a handful of ships have made it through. Before the war, it was about eighty-five a day. Tankers are still being attacked. And diesel in Europe still costs about seventy-seven percent more than before the war.
---
And we've actually seen this movie before. In nineteen seventy-three, Arab oil producers cut off oil to the U.S. Prices quadrupled. People waited hours in gas lines, and some states only let you fill up on odd or even days, based on your license plate. But here's the part that matters. That shock changed the world for decades. It's why America built its emergency oil reserve, and why cars got way more fuel efficient.
---
So here's my prediction. Even when the strait fully reopens, prices won't snap back fast. Tanker insurance stays expensive, and America's emergency reserve is at its lowest level since nineteen eighty-two, so governments will be buying millions of barrels to refill it. But the bigger change is long term. Just like nineteen seventy-three, this crisis pushes countries to build pipelines around Hormuz, store more oil at home, and switch faster to electric cars and solar. The world's oil highway won't stay the only road.
