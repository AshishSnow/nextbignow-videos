Facebook just got hit with an eight hundred and five million euro fine. That's almost nine hundred million dollars. The reason? Its app was looking at the photos on people's phones. Even ones they never posted. So what happens when apps can see everything in your gallery? Here's what happened. Since twenty twenty-three, Facebook's Android app had a feature that suggested photos and videos from your gallery to post. It was turned on by default. Italy's privacy watchdog says the app accessed people's photos, videos and their hidden data, without clear consent. About twenty million Android users in Italy were affected. And that hidden data matters. A photo isn't just a picture. It can carry the time it was taken, the phone that took it, and sometimes the exact location. That's how a single photo can show where you live. Meta says the fine is disproportionate and plans to appeal. It says the feature only works after users give permission, and they can switch it off any time. But Italy says one tap to allow gallery access isn't the same as clear consent for every use. And we've seen this before. In twenty eighteen, the world found out a political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica had pulled data from up to eighty-seven million Facebook users. Most of them never agreed to it. A year later, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission fined Facebook five billion dollars. At the time, the biggest privacy fine ever. So here's my prediction. Fines alone won't change much. Eight hundred million euros is less than half a percent of Meta's yearly revenue. What will change things is the order that comes with it. Meta has sixty days to ask for separate permission and keep the feature off by default. Expect more countries to copy that rule. And the simple fix for you? Open your phone's settings, check which apps can see all your photos, and switch them to selected photos only. And tell me. Do you let apps see all your photos? Yes or no, in the comments.
