In Spain right now, a young person who wants to rent a place alone needs ninety-nine percent of their paycheck. Ninety-nine percent. So what happens when a whole generation can't afford a home?
---
This week, Spain found out. On November eleventh, millions of workers walked off the job. It was the country's first general strike in fourteen years, and this time it wasn't really about pay. It was about rent. Unions say ports and car factories almost completely stopped. Madrid's regional government says far fewer people joined. Either way, the message was loud.
---
Here's how bad it got. Average rent in Spain has nearly doubled in ten years. A young worker makes about eleven hundred and ninety euros a month. The average apartment costs about eleven hundred and seventy-six. That's why three out of four Spaniards between sixteen and thirty-four still live with their parents. On average, they move out at thirty. Across Europe, it's twenty-six.
---
So why is this happening? Simple math. The Bank of Spain says the country is short about seven hundred and fifty thousand homes. Last year, it built just ninety-two thousand. More people, not enough homes, and prices only go one way.
---
And then came the story that broke people. In Madrid, an eighty-seven-year-old woman faced a rent five times higher than before. In September, she was evicted. Weeks later she died, and she became the face of this crisis.
---
Now it's shaking politics. Spain's prime minister called a snap election for November twenty-ninth, with housing right at the center of it.
---
So here's my prediction. Housing becomes the issue that decides elections, and not just in Spain. Expect more rent caps, more limits on tourist rentals, and governments promising to build fast. But here's the catch. Rent caps can help renters today, and they can also make people build less tomorrow. The only real fix is more homes, and homes take years.
