This week, the world's inbox just... stopped. Outlook, Teams, Microsoft three sixty-five. Down, for more than a day. So here's the real question. What happens next time? And why does one company's bad day break everything?
---
It started around noon, Eastern time, on November twelfth. At the peak, more than a hundred and thirty thousand people reported problems on Downdetector. And that's just the people who bothered to report it.
In the UK, schools, banks, hospitals, even a network used by Parliament were hit. Emails didn't vanish, but Microsoft had to hold them in a queue for up to forty-eight hours.
[surprised] And the cause? Not hackers. Microsoft says a bunch of its servers unexpectedly went offline, and the rest got crushed by the extra load. One part fails, and everything piled on top of it falls too.
---
Here's why that's scary. Most of the internet now lives inside just three companies. Amazon, Microsoft and Google run more than sixty percent of the world's cloud.
So when one of them sneezes, millions of businesses catch a cold. Your bank, your school, your airline. They don't own the system. They rent it.
---
And this keeps happening. July twenty twenty-four, one bad CrowdStrike update crashed about eight and a half million Windows computers. Flights were grounded. Hospitals went back to paper.
October twenty twenty-five, Amazon's cloud went down for about fifteen hours. Apps, games, even smart beds stopped working. [chuckle] Yes, beds.
---
So what's next? Here's my prediction. These outages are going to get bigger, not smaller. Because we're putting more and more of the world, now including AI, into the same few data centers.
Governments are already worried. In Europe, banks now have to prove they can survive if a big tech supplier fails. The UK is moving the same way.
And companies will start paying for a plan B. A backup cloud, a backup way to talk, even plain old paper.
---
That's why people fear a big system collapsing. You don't notice it... until the day it's gone.
