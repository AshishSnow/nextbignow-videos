For the first time in years, Wall Street is betting the Fed will raise interest rates again, not cut them. The ten-year Treasury yield just jumped above five percent. So what happens if the Fed starts hiking again? Here's what's going on. Inflation won't cool down. Core prices rose faster than expected in October, the third month in a row. Oil is pushing costs up. Right now, the Fed's rate sits between three point seven five and four percent. But one Fed governor just opened the door to a big half-point hike if inflation keeps speeding up. Why should you care? Because almost every loan in America follows these rates. Mortgages, car loans, credit cards. When the ten-year goes up, mortgage rates usually follow. And a house you could afford last year can suddenly cost hundreds more every month. We've seen the extreme version. In nineteen eighty, inflation hit almost fifteen percent. So Fed chair Paul Volcker pushed rates to around twenty percent. Mortgage rates went above eighteen percent. Angry home builders mailed him pieces of wood in protest. America fell into a deep recession, and unemployment hit almost eleven percent. But it worked. By nineteen eighty-three, inflation was down to around three percent. So here's my prediction. Nobody is going back to twenty percent. But if the Fed hikes even once or twice, the dream of cheap money is over for a while. Mortgages stay painful, stocks get shaky, and saving finally pays. If you have debt with a variable rate, now is the time to look at it. And no, this isn't financial advice. And tell me. Should the Fed raise rates? Yes or no, in the comments.
