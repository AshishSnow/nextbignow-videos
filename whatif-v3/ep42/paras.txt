What if you were trapped seven hundred meters underground... and nobody up top knew if you were even alive? That's exactly what happened to thirty-three miners in Chile. And they were stuck down there for sixty-nine days.
It was August fifth, twenty ten, at a copper and gold mine in the Atacama Desert. Around two in the afternoon, the mountain above them collapsed. A giant slab of rock sealed the only way out.
---
The men made it to a small emergency shelter, deep in the mine. The problem? It had food for maybe two or three days. And there were thirty-three of them.
So they made a rule. Every two days, each man got two spoonfuls of tuna, a sip of milk, and a bite of a cracker. [surprised] That's it. Every two days.
It was hot, around thirty-five degrees, and super humid. They drank dirty water from the machines. Each man lost about eight kilos.
---
Up on the surface, rescuers were drilling blind. Thin holes, hundreds of meters deep, just hoping to hit the right spot.
On day seventeen, one drill finally broke through near them. The miners banged on the drill with tools, and taped a little note to it.
[happy] When the drill came back up, the note said: we are fine in the shelter, the thirty-three. The whole country went crazy.
---
But here's the thing. Finding them was one thing. Getting them out was another. Engineers said a rescue tunnel could take months.
So, while they waited, everything came down through that tiny hole. Food, water, medicine, letters from home, even a tiny projector. They called the delivery tubes the doves.
---
Then, on October twelfth, a rescue shaft was ready. And the ride up was in a steel capsule called the Phoenix. It was barely wider than a person's shoulders.
[dramatic] One by one, each man squeezed inside and rode up through the rock. Every trip took somewhere between nine and eighteen minutes.
They came out wearing sunglasses, because after more than two months in the dark, sunlight could hurt their eyes.
---
[happy] Twenty-two hours later, all thirty-three were out. Every single one of them. Millions of people around the world watched it live.
The last man up was the shift boss, Luis Urzua. He was the one who kept everyone going down there.
