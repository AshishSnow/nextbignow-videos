Twelve years earlier, he had sold KFC for two million dollars. Seven years after that, it sold again, for two hundred and eighty five million. So how does a founder end up hating his own chicken? It comes down to one deal he made at seventy three.

Harland Sanders did not start young. He spent his first forty years bouncing between jobs. Railroads. Insurance. He even practiced law, until he got into a fight in court with his own client.

Then, around nineteen thirty, he got a gas station in Corbin, Kentucky. Truck drivers kept asking where to eat, so he put his family's dining table in the front room and started cooking for them.

People came back for the food. So he bought the motel across the street, with a restaurant that seated a hundred and forty two people. In nineteen thirty five, the governor made him an honorary Kentucky colonel. It wasn't a military rank, but he took it seriously. He started calling himself the Colonel, and later he started wearing the white suit. He was turning himself into the brand, years before there was a brand. And in nineteen thirty nine, he started frying chicken in a pressure cooker, with his eleven herbs and spices. Fast, crispy, and nobody else had it.

He finally had a hit. Then the road moved.

A new interstate was planned around Corbin, and the traffic that fed his restaurant was about to disappear. In nineteen fifty six, he sold the place for seventy five thousand dollars, less than half of what it was worth. He was sixty five, living on a one hundred and five dollar Social Security check.

So he went on the road. He drove from restaurant to restaurant, cooked his chicken for the owners, and asked for a small fee on every piece they sold. Most said no. In his first two years, only five signed up.

But one of his first partners, Pete Harman in Salt Lake City, had already found the trick. He sold the chicken in a bucket, and called it Kentucky Fried Chicken. Families bought it by the bucket. By nineteen sixty four, there were more than six hundred restaurants across the United States and Canada. And the face on every sign was his.

And that's when two businessmen, John Y. Brown and Jack Massey, made him an offer.

Two million dollars for the company. Plus forty thousand dollars a year for life, to keep being the face of the brand. He was seventy three. He was tired of the road. And he thought he was keeping the part that mattered. His face, and his recipe. He said yes.

The new owners moved fast. By nineteen seventy one, there were about three thousand five hundred franchises. That year, just seven years after buying it, they sold the company to a food giant called Heublein, for two hundred and eighty five million dollars.

The Colonel got none of that. But the money was not what made him angry. It was the cooking. The man on the bucket started saying out loud what he thought of the food.

That's how he ended up in that Manhattan KFC in nineteen seventy six. After the worst chicken line, he went after the gravy. You add some of this brown gravy stuff, he said, and then you have sludge.

He even sued the company that owned his name and face, for one hundred and twenty two million dollars. They settled out of court for about one million.

The Colonel died in nineteen eighty. Today there are more than thirty thousand KFC restaurants, and his face is still on the bucket. The man who complained the loudest is the one selling the chicken.

When you sell a company, you are not selling the past. You are selling every decision after it. He kept his face, but he gave away the say. He sold the company. He never sold the standard.
