# The Economics of Owning a Bus Business (US + Europe) — script for approval


## INTRO
Right now, somewhere on a highway, about fifty people are asleep on a night bus.
Headphones on, seats back. Nobody is thinking about money.
Except one person. The owner.
Because that bus can cost more than a house, and every mile eats cash.
So is owning buses actually a good business?
Today we do the math.
What a bus costs, how it earns, and where the money leaks.
And why Europe's top long-distance bus brand grew without owning buses.

## 1. WHAT A COACH REALLY COSTS
Let's start with the machine itself.
A full-size intercity coach, the kind with fifty-plus seats, is not cheap.
Brand new, you're looking at more than half a million dollars.
One American operator paid about six hundred thousand per bus.
Most owners don't pay cash. They finance it, often over five to seven years.
So before a single passenger climbs on, there's a big payment due every month.
Used coaches cost far less. That's how many small operators get started.
And there are more of these out there than you'd think.
Nearly fifty thousand motorcoaches, in the US alone.
And fewer than two thousand companies run all of them.
So this isn't one giant's game. Most bus companies run fewer than ten coaches.

## 2. THREE WAYS A BUS MAKES MONEY
Now the fun part. How does a bus actually make money?
There are three main ways, and each one carries a very different risk.
Way number one. Charter.
A school trip, a wedding, or a company retreat rents the whole bus.
Pricing guides put a full-size coach at a hundred-plus dollars an hour.
A full day can easily run past a thousand dollars.
The upside? The customer pays for the whole bus, full or empty.
The catch? You have to keep finding the next customer, every single week.
Way number two. Your own scheduled route.
You pick two cities, set a timetable, and sell seats one by one.
When the bus is full, it's great money.
When it's half empty, you still pay for the whole trip.
Fuel doesn't care who's on board.
Way number three. Contracts.
A university shuttle, an airport run, or a company moving its workers every day.
Often you're paid for the bus's time, full or not.
Less upside per trip, but much less risk. That's the steady money.

## 3. THE FLIXBUS MODEL
And then there's a fourth way. Let someone else sell your seats.
To see how that works, we need to take a little trip to Germany.
Until twenty thirteen, the country limited long-distance buses to protect its railway.
When that rule changed, three childhood friends were ready. Their first buses ran within weeks.
And they weren't alone. Within a year, the number of intercity bus routes nearly tripled.
Prices crashed. Some bus fares were about half the train price.
In a few years, one startup bought or merged with its biggest rivals.
Their company was called FlixBus, and here's the twist that made it work.
They barely owned any buses.
Local bus companies own the coaches and employ the drivers.
Flix plans the routes, sets the prices, runs the app, and sells the tickets.
Then it keeps a cut of each ticket. Reports put it around a quarter.
Think of it like Uber, but for intercity buses.
For the bus owner, that means selling seats without running their own marketing.
For Flix, it means growing fast without buying whole fleets.
In twenty twenty-one, Flix even bought Greyhound, the famous American bus line.
That deal brought in about thirteen hundred Greyhound vehicles.
So the company that grew without owning buses suddenly owned a fleet.
In twenty twenty-three, it carried about eighty-one million passengers.
Revenue passed two billion euros.
Now the part people miss. Its adjusted profit margin was only about five percent.
And that's before interest, taxes, and buses slowly wearing out.
Even the giant of the industry lives on thin margins.

## 4. WHERE THE MONEY GOES
So let's follow the money on one long trip, and see where it goes.
Bill number one is fuel. The one that never stops.
Say your coach gets around six miles per gallon on the highway.
A five-hundred-mile run burns more than eighty gallons of diesel.
And right now, US diesel averages over six dollars a gallon.
Do the math. That's around five hundred dollars of fuel, one way.
Bill number two. The driver.
The typical US transit and intercity bus driver earns about fifty-nine thousand a year.
And good drivers have been hard to find.
One recent industry survey found about one in five needed drivers missing.
Bill number three. Insurance.
Run a bus built for sixteen or more, and the feds demand big insurance.
At least five million dollars of liability.
Then come tires, brakes, repairs, a place to park it, and the loan payment.
Let's put it together with a rough, made-up example. Easy numbers.
Fifty seats, forty dollars a ticket, every seat sold.
That's two thousand dollars for the trip.
If a booking platform keeps a quarter, you're down to fifteen hundred.
Take out about five hundred for fuel, and maybe a few hundred for the driver.
You're left with roughly seven hundred, before the loan, insurance, and repairs.
Not bad. Now picture the exact same trip, with only half the seats sold.
The fuel and the driver cost you exactly the same as before. Profit? Basically gone.
That's the whole business in one sentence. Empty seats are where the money disappears.
And here's the scary part. A bus sitting in the yard still costs money.
The loan, the insurance, and the parking keep running, even when it earns nothing.
So owners obsess over one thing. Keep the bus moving, and the seats full.

## 5. THE RULES (US + EUROPE)
Now, the paperwork. You can't just buy a bus and start driving.
In the US, you need federal operating authority to carry passengers across state lines.
Every driver needs a commercial license with a passenger endorsement.
And since late twenty sixteen, new motorcoaches have had to come with seat belts.
Europe has its own rulebook.
A coach driver can usually drive nine hours a day.
After four and a half hours behind the wheel, they need a break. Forty-five minutes.
And a little box called a tachograph records all of it.
These rules cost owners time and money. They also keep passengers safe.

## 6. THE VERDICT
So, is owning buses a good business?
The upside? People always need to travel, and one bus moves a lot of them.
For a lot of trips, a coach ticket costs less than flying or driving alone.
The catch? Big money up front, thin margins, and one empty month really hurts.
And when too many buses chase one route, fares can drop fast.
Honestly? The winners aren't always the ones with the most buses.
They're the ones who keep every bus busy.

## 7. HOW YOU WOULD START
So if you wanted in, how would you start? Three moves.
One. Start with one good used coach, not a shiny new fleet.
Two. Lock in contracts first. Steady hours are what pay the loan.
Three. Plug into a booking network instead of building your own app.
And know your cost per mile before you sign anything.
